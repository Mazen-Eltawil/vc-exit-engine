    How the engine decides

    Every number on a position page comes from the steps below. All of them run in your browser on the inputs you entered, with no hidden data source.

    1. Today's value of the company

    A 2021 round price is not today's value. The engine follows IPEV calibration: start from the last round, then move it with market evidence.

    • OPM backsolve (AICPA valuation guide). Find the equity value at which the last round's preferred shares, with their preference and conversion rights, are worth exactly their issue price. This sits below the headline post-money, because preferred stock is worth more than common.
    • Index roll-forward. Move that value by the comparable public index since the round.
    • Revenue multiple. Revenue × public comps multiple × (1 − private discount) + cash − debt.
    • Blend. The weight on the round decays as it ages: w = exp(−age / 1.5 years). A five-year-old round carries 4% weight.

    2. What your shares receive at any exit

    The full liquidation waterfall: preferences paid by seniority (pari passu within a rank), participation with caps, and each class converting to common when that pays more. Classes convert in order of their threshold, which is the equilibrium. In a qualified IPO every class converts. Fair value is the option pricing method: the waterfall averaged over a risk-neutral lognormal exit value.

    • Runway. With under nine months of cash the company tries to raise. Success odds rise with its Rule of 40, an open window and momentum since the last round. Each new round dilutes you. A down round triggers anti-dilution and comes in senior. Run out of cash with no round and the company is sold for 10 to 40% of its value.
    • Exits as competing risks. IPO hazard switches on with scale (revenue above the IPO bar) and quality (Rule of 40), scaled by the window. Acquisition hazard rises with quality and the window. IPO proceeds carry six months of lock-up risk. Acquisitions pay a control premium averaging 10%.
    • Disruption. A shock knocks 25 points off growth and 40% off the multiple.

    4. Uncertainty about the model itself

    Paths are split into 16 assumption sets. Each draws its own growth decay, margin path, multiple sensitivity, comps level, exit hazards and financing odds from priors centred on your inputs (Bayesian model averaging). The spread of answers across sets is the "P10 to P90" band and the agreement count.

    5. When to sell, if not now

    Every six months the engine compares the after-tax secondary bid with the expected value of waiting. That continuation value comes from a least-squares regression on the state: the bid level, Rule of 40, exit window and runway. This is Longstaff–Schwartz, the method used for American options. Holding is valued under this optimal resale policy, at your hurdle rate.

    6. The price test

    Sell Ratio = net bid today, after fees, costs, carry and tax / value of holding at your hurdle

    Above 1, the bid wins. When some assumption sets favour selling and others holding, the engine sells the fraction that minimises the worst-case regret between them (Manski's diversification under ambiguity). With fees in proportion to the price that fraction is f = (bid − V_P10) / (V_P90 − V_P10). A minimum fee or upfront costs make small sales dearer, so each fraction is tested directly. The price ladder inverts this. Below the walk-away price sell nothing. Above the sell-all price sell everything.

    Sale costs apply to every sale, today's and later ones: the bank or broker fee with its minimum, costs paid even if no sale closes, and the months until the cash arrives, discounted at your hurdle. Today's upfront costs count once per expected attempt: at a 60% chance of closing, 1.7 times.

    7. The concentration test

    Family wealth at the horizon is simulated path by path. The rest of the portfolio and any cash from this stake compound along the same market draws, so correlation with public markets is counted. Each sale fraction is scored by constant-relative-risk-aversion utility, then aggregated across assumption sets with smooth ambiguity aversion (Klibanoff, Marinacci and Mukerji). This test can raise the sale fraction for a large or very volatile stake. It never blocks a sale that passes the price test.

    8. Taxes and frictions

    • Bank or broker fees on secondary sales, with any minimum fee, costs paid even if no sale closes, and the months until the cash arrives. SPV carry on gains above cost.
    • Federal long-term rate including the 3.8% net investment income tax, plus a state rate.
    • QSBS (§1202): once held five years, gains up to the greater of $10M or 10× basis are excluded. The test applies at each sale date, so waiting can earn the exclusion. You can set whether the state follows it.
    • Selling at a loss creates a tax credit when the family has gains to offset.

    9. What the engine does not know

    • Private information about the company: a pending acquisition offer, a key customer at risk. Fold it in through growth, the hazards or the disruption rate.
    • Which buyers exist. The buyer count and the spread of their prices are placeholders until the bank reports real interest.
    • Fund LP interests. This version values direct stakes and SPV holdings in one company.
    • Tax advice. QSBS eligibility and state treatment need your tax counsel.

    10. Selling through a bank: buyers and the package

    A marketed sale draws a random number of serious buyers (a Poisson count, two on average unless you enter the bank's count). Each bids around the name's typical price, with the spread you enter or the spread of three or more bids you have. The typical secondary discount is read as where deals usually clear, so the usual field of two buyers reproduces that price, and only a busier or quieter field moves it. The best bid, or a bid in hand if higher, sells when it reaches the reserve: the walk-away price. From this the engine gives each stake a chance of a sale at or above the reserve, a chance of no offers, a likely price, and the expected gain over holding. "Where to focus the bank" ranks stakes by that gain, and charges the engagement's minimum fee and retainer once to the package.

    11. Where the assumptions come from

    Three kinds of number feed every answer. Facts come from your documents: revenue, cash, the cap table, cost, bids. Policy is the family's choice: hurdle, risk aversion, tax, the bank's terms. Behaviour is the rest: how fast growth fades, how often companies are bought or go public, how buyers price stakes. Behaviour starts at engine defaults. They are reasonable for older US venture companies, but they are not fitted to data. Each position's "What moves the answer" test reruns the engine with every assumption pushed lower and higher, tags each one as your data, a family setting or an engine default, and names the defaults that move the answer most. Replace those first. Testing showed that errors in shared assumptions push every answer the same way: the 16 assumption sets do not cancel them. Calibration on the family's own past exits and secondary quotes is the fix.

    12. How this relates to the 100-point model

    Every criterion of the original scoring model is measured on each position page. Liquidity and an identifiable buyer (25 points) is the chance of a sale at or above the reserve, the chance of no offers, and whether the transfer can close. Exit price (20) is the Sell Ratio. Upside against downside (20) is the simulated range of outcomes. Revenue trajectory (7) and round recency (3) drive the simulation and the value. Holding period (15) and MOIC (10) enter through tax and the planning horizon, not as reasons to sell: an old stake is not worth less for being old, and the price paid does not change what it is worth now. The engine gives no 0 to 100 score. Fixed points cannot say whether a price beats holding, and in testing the score had almost no link to the true gain from selling.
